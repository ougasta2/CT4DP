\section{Discussion}
Ridge's lower 2024 MAE coincided with a higher RMSE and a lower eligible hit
rate (17.80\% versus 20.35\%). A typical reduction in absolute error does not
guarantee fewer extreme misses or more forecasts within a tight band. This
motivates a separate analysis of where large errors occur.

The autumn-evening association persisted into 2024. Its lift cannot be compared
directly with the cluster rule to measure the contribution of a cluster label:
the rules describe different calendar conditions. The afternoon comparison
holds time of day fixed and shows no enrichment for the selected cluster in
2024, with substantial uncertainty. Seasons and other conditions may still
differ between the two sets of days. The comparison concerns a single selected
rule, and the Davies--Bouldin index does not measure whether regimes explain
forecast error. Sparse regimes and temporal dependence further limit inference.

Both years informed earlier work, so 2024 is a chronological evaluation rather
than an untouched test. The bootstrap interval is conditional on the rule and
threshold; it does not account for candidate selection or establish causality.
Unverified source publication or revision times may affect how closely the
benchmarked inputs reproduce those available at the intended forecasting cutoff.

Lasso reduced MAE relative to Ridge by 4.6\% in 2023 and 2.7\% in
2024, while RMSE fell by 1.9\% and 0.6\%, respectively. The smaller
RMSE improvement in 2024 indicates that the aggregate gain was limited.
Both models use the same inputs and a linear specification; Lasso
additionally selects predictors through its sparsity-inducing penalty. Its errors closely track Ridge's (hourly correlation 0.98), and it beat Ridge
in 11 of 12 months in each year, so the gain is consistent but small. For the
same reason, the transfer of the autumn-evening rule to Lasso is weaker evidence
of model independence than its transfer to gradient boosting.

Lasso achieved the lowest MAE and RMSE among the five models in 2023.
In 2024, however, its MAE and RMSE exceeded gradient boosting's by
1.26 and 1.88 EUR/MWh, respectively. Unlike the linear models,
gradient boosting can represent nonlinear relationships and interactions.
Its advantage was not uniform: Lasso had lower negative-price MAE
in 2024 (19.79 versus 20.69 EUR/MWh).

For the Random Forest, the highest prior-day price regime (regime 3) had
the largest large-error rates in both years (16.67\% and 13.43\%).
This held when the spike regime was split at \(k=5\). However, regime 3
covers only 11 and 18 days and we computed no interval, so this is a
suggestive association in development years, not evidence that the
regime explains forecast error.

For gradient boosting, the autumn-evening rule transferred: its 2024 lift
(2.19) was higher than for autumn alone (1.55) or evening alone (1.40), so the
combination adds to its two parts, and the pattern is not specific to Ridge.
The cluster rule did not transfer. In 2023 its boosting lift (1.43) matched
that of afternoon hours alone (1.42), so the cluster label added nothing beyond
the time band, and in 2024 cluster-0 afternoons had fewer large errors than
other afternoons, although the interval includes zero. The highest-lift rules
selected on the 2023 boosting errors also failed in 2024, so a high lift in
the validation year was a poor guide to which rules persist. These are
associations between conditions and large errors: they show where the
forecasts miss, not that adding regime labels as inputs would reduce
errors, which we did not test.
