\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the model comparison shows}
The ranking depends on which errors matter. Ridge's lower MAE in
2024 coincided with higher RMSE and fewer eligible hits, consistent
with larger misses contributing more to the squared-error metric.
Boosting led the annual comparison in 2024, yet Ridge was more
accurate on negative-price hours. Many prices near zero are excluded
from the relative hit metric, so a higher hit rate offers limited
evidence about performance there. A user concerned with negative prices
would need the corresponding subgroup scores when choosing a model.

Lasso retained about 77 of 604 inputs per hour model in the pre-2023
fit. Its gains came with more training time, about 140 seconds for
24 refits against 3 seconds for Ridge on the same laptop. Since
price lags can be correlated, sparse coefficients are useful for
prediction and give limited evidence about the importance of any
single lag. MLP tuning improved on its starter in both years, with
most of the remaining gap to Ridge concentrated overnight and in the
late evening. Longer iteration budgets barely changed either
starter's validation errors, suggesting that the observed gaps
did not mainly result from stopping the optimizer too early.

\subsection{Transfer of calendar and regime rules}
Autumn-evening enrichment persisted across linear, tree-based, and
neural forecasts. Lasso and Ridge had highly correlated hourly
errors (0.98), so their agreement is closely related. Transfer to
the other model families broadens the evidence, although all five
models share the same inputs and delivery hours. Lift compares
relative enrichment: boosting recorded fewer autumn-evening
events than Ridge under its own threshold and still had higher lift
because its annual event rate was lower. The common-threshold
comparison provides a check on this dependence.

The selected cluster rule provided little enrichment within
afternoons. Holding time of day fixed reduces one source of
composition differences, while season can still differ between
groups. The Ridge interval spans both a lower and a higher rate
for cluster 0, so the data give little basis for using that label
as a warning flag. The Davies--Bouldin score characterizes
separation in input space. A well-separated regime can still have
an imprecise forecast-error estimate when it covers few days.
The highest prior-price regime illustrates the trade-off: it
contained difficult hours for forest and boosting, yet its
frequency fell below the 5\% support requirement. Lowering support
would admit rarer patterns and increase the need for separate
validation.

\subsection{What a later evaluation would test}
Both years informed earlier work, and the block interval is
conditional on the rules and thresholds selected. Uncertain
historical release times also limit claims about the operational
10:00 cutoff. A prospective evaluation could freeze the rules,
archive the inputs available at issue time, and measure how many
errors the autumn-evening flag catches at a given alert volume.
This would test usefulness under later market conditions and
show whether calendar enrichment persists across another cycle.
